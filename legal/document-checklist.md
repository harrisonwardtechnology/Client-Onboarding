# Document Checklist

The papers that should exist before work starts. Tick them off. Chase the missing ones.

**Not legal advice.** This is a list of what usually exists and why it matters.

---

## Before any information is shared

- [ ] **Mutual NDA.** Covers both directions. You will see their secrets, and they will see your methods and pricing
  - *Watch for:* a one way NDA that only protects them, and a term so long it is unenforceable
  - *Watch for:* a definition of confidential information broad enough to cover things you already knew

## Before work starts

- [ ] **Master Services Agreement, or a services agreement**
  - *Watch for:* **unlimited liability.** Cap it, usually at fees paid. This is the single most important clause for a small firm
  - *Watch for:* **indemnity clauses** that make you responsible for their breach
  - *Watch for:* **IP assignment** that hands over your templates and methods, not just the deliverable
  - *Watch for:* **non-solicit** clauses that stop you hiring anyone, forever, anywhere
  - *Watch for:* **auto renewal** with a short notice window

- [ ] **Statement of Work.** Specific deliverables, dates, price, payment terms
  - *Watch for:* deliverables described as outcomes you cannot control ("client will be compliant")
  - *Watch for:* payment on acceptance with no definition of acceptance
  - *Watch for:* no change control process. Add one

- [ ] **Insurance certificates.** Usually they want yours. Professional liability and cyber
  - *Watch for:* required limits higher than you carry. Find out before signing, not at invoice time

- [ ] **Data Processing Agreement**, if you will touch personal data
  - Needed under GDPR and increasingly asked for regardless
  - *Watch for:* being named as a processor when you are actually just an advisor who never touches data. If you do not process, say so

- [ ] **Business Associate Agreement**, if health data is involved and you might see it
  - *Watch for:* signing one when you do not need to. It adds real obligations

- [ ] **Authorization letter**, if there is any testing, scanning, or probing
  - See `authorization-letter-template.md`. Verbal permission is not permission

## Nice to have, avoids pain later

- [ ] **Named contacts document.** Who approves, who escalates, who to call at 2am
- [ ] **Change control note template.** So the first scope change is a form, not an argument
- [ ] **Subcontractor disclosure.** If you use anyone else, say so before they find out

---

## Clauses worth pushing back on, and how

You will not win every one. Pick two.

| Clause | Why it hurts | What to ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Unlimited liability | One mistake ends your business | Cap at fees paid, or a multiple of fees |
| Broad indemnity | You cover their losses from their own systems | Limit to your negligence, exclude their acts |
| Full IP assignment | You lose the templates you brought with you | You keep pre-existing IP and methods, they own the deliverable |
| Perpetual non-solicit | Blocks normal hiring for years | 12 months, limited to people who worked on the engagement |
| Acceptance not defined | They can withhold payment indefinitely | Deemed accepted after 10 business days without written objection |
| No cap on revisions | Endless rewrites | Two rounds of revisions included, further rounds billed |

**How to push back without friction:**

> "Two things I need to change before I can sign. The liability cap and the IP clause. Everything else is fine. Can I send redlines today?"

Naming two and accepting the rest reads as reasonable. It usually gets both.

---

## If they will not negotiate

Some large companies genuinely cannot change their standard terms. Then decide:

- Is the fee big enough to justify the risk?
- Can you buy insurance that covers the exposure?
- Can you narrow what you actually do, so the risky clause has less to bite?

Walking away is a valid answer. Signing unlimited liability for a small fee is not.
