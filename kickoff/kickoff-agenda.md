# Kickoff Agenda

Ninety minutes. Everyone who matters. No slide deck longer than five slides.

**Attendees:** sponsor, internal owner, IT lead, and anyone whose team you will need. Legal and HR if the work touches people or contracts.

---

## Before the meeting

- [ ] Intake forms received and read
- [ ] Three specific questions written down from their own answers
- [ ] Access requested, and status known
- [ ] Agenda sent 48 hours ahead
- [ ] Confirmed the sponsor is coming

---

## The ninety minutes

### 0 to 10. Why we are here

The sponsor speaks, not you. Ask them to say in their own words what problem this is solving and why now.

**Why:** the room needs to hear it from them, not from the consultant. It sets the authority for everything after.

If the sponsor cannot articulate it, that is your first finding and you have learned it in the first ten minutes.

### 10 to 20. What we are doing, and what we are not

Read the scope out loud. Both halves. Especially the out of scope half.

**Why:** the out of scope list is what stops eleven small favours later. Everyone hearing it at once means nobody can claim they did not know.

### 20 to 35. What done looks like

Play back their definition from discovery. Ask if it is still right.

Write the answer on the screen where everyone can see it, and put it at the top of every status report from then on.

**Why:** definitions drift. Anchoring it in public in week one gives you something to point at in week nine.

### 35 to 50. Who does what

Walk the RACI. Name every owner out loud. Get verbal confirmation from each named person while everyone is listening.

**Why:** people accept ownership more readily in a room than in a spreadsheet. Silence in a meeting is easier to notice than silence in email.

### 50 to 60. Access and information

Say exactly what you need, when, and from whom. Confirm the read only principle out loud.

> "To be clear, I am asking for read access only. I will not be changing anything. If a change is ever needed I will come to [IT lead] and ask, in writing, with a reason."

**Why:** this sentence removes most of the resistance you would otherwise spend three weeks fighting.

### 60 to 70. How we will talk

Walk `communication-norms.md`. Confirm the checkpoint dates and put them in calendars before people leave the room.

**Why:** meetings scheduled in the meeting actually happen. Meetings scheduled by email later do not.

### 70 to 85. What could go wrong

Ask the room directly:

> "What is most likely to slow this down?"

Then shut up and let them answer. Write it down.

**Why:** they know. They always know. Someone will name the person who never responds, the system nobody can get into, or the deadline that is not real. This is the most valuable fifteen minutes of the meeting.

### 85 to 90. First three actions

Name three things, each with an owner and a date, happening in the next week. Small ones.

**Why:** momentum. An engagement that produces nothing in week one is already behind.

---

## Within 24 hours after

Send a short recap. Not minutes. Five bullets:

1. What done looks like, in their words
2. What is in scope, one line. What is out of scope, one line
3. Named owners
4. The three actions, with dates
5. The risks the room named, and who owns each

**Why:** this email is the reference document for the whole engagement. Write it well and you will point at it a dozen times.

---

## Things that go wrong at kickoff, and what to do

| What happens | What to do |
|---|---|
| Sponsor does not show | Reschedule. Do not proceed |
| Someone challenges the scope | Good. Resolve it now, in the room, in writing |
| Someone is clearly hostile | Talk to them alone afterward. Usually they are being asked to do this on top of a full job |
| Nobody speaks | Ask the quietest technical person a direct, specific question. They usually know the most |
| It turns into a technical deep dive | Park it. "Let me take that offline, it deserves more than five minutes" |
| The scope is already wrong | Say so out loud, now. Week one is the cheapest time to fix it |
